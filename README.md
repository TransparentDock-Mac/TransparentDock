## The one part of the desktop you never got to pick

There is a mild absurdity in spending an hour choosing a wallpaper and then covering its bottom edge with a
tray you had no say in. TransparentDock treats that tray as adjustable furniture. Transparency runs the
full range — fully transparent at one end, fully opaque at the other, and anything you like in between —
and around that sit further options for the dock's appearance and functionality, each of which can be
switched on by itself or folded together into a look you keep.

## Three things it changes about the shelf

- **Transparency becomes a dial rather than a fact.** Set the dock to vanish entirely, to sit at half
  strength, or anywhere along the way, and see the wallpaper you paid attention to underneath it.
- **Appearance options apply independently or in combination.** Use one on its own, or gather several into
  a single theme that carries all of them at once.
- **Nothing you do is one-way.** Built-in reset options return the dock to its original state, so the worst
  outcome of an experiment is a few seconds spent undoing it.

## Themes are the part that makes it stick

A single tweak is a novelty; it stops being interesting after a week. What keeps this installed is the
customizable palette, which saves an unlimited number of custom dock themes and lets you switch between
them. That turns dock appearance into something contextual rather than fixed. The look you want while
working through a dark evening is not the look you want in a bright room, and it is certainly not the look
you want on a projector in front of forty people. With themes saved, each of those is a choice you make
once and recall in a click.

Build them around situations rather than around settings. A theme called *demo* that is nearly opaque and
completely plain earns its keep the first time you share a screen, because the alternative is dragging a
transparency control back to sensible while people watch.

## Reset deserves more credit than it gets

The reset option is easy to read past in a feature list and it is quietly the reason the whole thing is
low-risk. Customisation tools that cannot cleanly undo themselves make people conservative — you try one
safe change and never touch it again. Knowing the dock can be put back exactly as macOS shipped it does
the opposite: you try the extreme setting, decide it is too much, and go back without hunting through
preferences or reinstalling anything.

## Who ends up glad they bought it

People who look at their desktop for eight hours a day, mostly. Anyone with a wallpaper they chose
deliberately and would rather not have obscured. Anyone whose screen appears in recordings or shared
sessions and who wants a cleaner, quieter version of it available on demand. It is not a productivity
purchase and does not pretend to be — it is a comfort one, aimed at the part of the screen you cannot stop
seeing.
